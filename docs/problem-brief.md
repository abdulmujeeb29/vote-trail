# Problem brief: VoteTrail
*Every Vote, Every Voice · KTech Fest 3.0*

## Amaka's election
Amaka lives in Garki, Abuja. She registered, collected her voter's card, and on election morning she walked to her polling unit.

**It wasn't there.** It had moved weeks earlier. INEC had posted a notice online, but Amaka doesn't browse INEC's website, and nobody told her.

**When she found the new one,** the queue was long and the officials were late. Nobody outside the unit knew why, or when voting would start.

**That evening,** the results were pasted on the wall and people took photos. Days later, when the final numbers were announced, people asked: *is that what we saw on the wall?* There was no way to check.

**Meanwhile,** a WhatsApp message said votes were being bought for ₦5,000. It was forwarded forty times before anyone asked if it was true.

Amaka is not one person. Each of these things happened to real voters in 2023.

## The weak link
**INEC publishes the information. Voters don't get it, and can't check it.**

INEC already puts most of the election online: registration, polling unit changes, and photos of every polling unit's result sheet on its results website (IReV). But that information:
- **waits for you to find it.** Nothing tells you when something changes;
- **is in English, for smartphones,** in a country where many voters have neither;
- **is never compared with what people saw.** The result on the wall and the result online sit in different places.

When information doesn't reach people and can't be checked, rumours fill the gap. **A count can be correct and still not be trusted.**

## Why it matters now
- **Moved units:** EU observers reported voters weren't told in time that their polling units had moved (2023).
- **Late starts:** some units opened up to **five hours late**, or not at all (EU observers, 2023).
- **Results:** only about **1 in 5** presidential results was online by noon the next day.
- **Rumours:** election misinformation rose, and fake AI-made audio and images are expected next time.
- **Next election:** **16 January 2027**, with **over 103 million** registered voters.

## Who it hurts
| Who | Today |
|---|---|
| **Voters**, especially basic-phone users, non-English speakers, older people and people with disabilities | Miss changes, can't check results, believe or spread rumours |
| **Polling officials** | Blamed for delays they can't explain to anyone |
| **Party agents and observers** | Photograph the result on the wall, with no way to compare or prove it |
| **Community leaders** | Asked questions they can't answer |
| **INEC** | Loses trust even when its process works |

## Our answer: VoteTrail
**VoteTrail tells every voter what changed, in their language, on any phone, and lets them check what was counted.**

| What happened to Amaka | What VoteTrail does |
|---|---|
| Her unit moved | She gets an SMS and a voice note, in Pidgin, weeks before. Basic phone? She dials a short code; no data needed. |
| Officials were late | Officials tap a short code when they arrive, so everyone can see "Open since 09:40". |
| She doubted the result | Agents photograph the wall result. AI reads the handwriting and compares it with INEC's website. If two people from **rival parties** both show a difference, it's flagged, with an evidence pack. |
| The rumour spread | She pastes the message. Forty forwards still count as **one** source. She gets a clear answer, with reasons, in her language. |

Works in **English, Pidgin, Hausa, Yoruba and Igbo**: in the app, by USSD and SMS, and by voice.

## Why people can trust it
- **AI reads, rules decide.** AI only reads handwriting, messages and notices. Fixed, tested rules decide every status.
- **Two independent people, or no alarm.** One photo can't trigger a mismatch. Forwards are never proof.
- **VoteTrail never says who won.** Only INEC declares results.

## How it grows
- **Uses what's already there:** INEC's own notices and results website, the phones people have, and the steps officials and agents already take.
- **Cheap to run:** one server handles hundreds of uploads at once; a viral rumour is checked once, not forty times.
- **Who pays:** observer groups and fact-checkers carry SMS costs, and telcos can make the short code free. Voters pay nothing.

## What success looks like
- Voters hear about a moved unit **before** election day, not at the gate.
- Every polling unit result can be **checked by citizens** against INEC's own upload.
- A rumour gets a clear answer **within minutes**, in the voter's language.
- **No new burden:** officials and agents use the phones and steps they already have.
